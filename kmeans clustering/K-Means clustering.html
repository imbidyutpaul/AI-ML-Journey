Part 1: unsupervised learning

Supervised learning has features (f1, f2, …) and an output column (the dependent feature) that you predict: linear regression, logistic regression, decision trees, boosting, Random Forest and SVM all do this. In unsupervised learning there is no output column. You are not predicting anything; you look for structure in the features. The most common task is clustering: put similar rows in the same group.

Three common clustering methods

  • K-Means: Divides data into a chosen number (k) of clusters by grouping points around their nearest centroid.
  • Hierarchical clustering: Builds a hierarchy of clusters by repeatedly merging nearby groups or splitting larger ones.
  • DBSCAN: Groups closely packed points based on data density and marks isolated points as noise or outliers.

Where clustering is used

  • Customer segmentation (the video’s example, below).
  • Document or news grouping by topic.
  • Image colour compression: replace millions of colours with k representative ones.
  • Anomaly spotting: points far from every centroid are unusual.

Example: the iPhone discount

An online store has two features per customer: salary and spending score (1 to 10). It is launching a ₹1.5 lakh phone and wants to decide who gets a discount. No “right answer” column exists, so it clusters the customers.

Each group gets its own offer (the video mentions discounts such as 5%, 10% and 20%) instead of one blanket discount. K-Means is one of three clustering techniques in the series, next to hierarchical clustering and DBSCAN; this guide covers only K-Means.

Part 2: the K-Means idea

K-Means groups points into k clusters. Each cluster is summarised by its centre, the centroid (the mean of its points). A point belongs to the cluster whose centroid is nearest. The name says it all: K clusters, each defined by a mean.

TermMeaning
kNumber of clusters you ask for
CentroidMean position of a cluster’s points
AssignmentWhich centroid each point is closest to
WCSS / inertiaSum of squared distances from points to their own centroid
ConvergenceCentroids stop moving between iterations

The geometry: perpendicular bisectors

Between any two centroids, the points equally far from both lie on a line: the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them. Everything on one side joins one centroid, everything on the other side joins the other. With several centroids these lines carve the plane into regions (a Voronoi diagram). Part 3: the algorithm, step by step

  1. Initialise k centroids.
  2. Assign every point to its nearest centroid.
  3. Update each centroid to the mean of its assigned points.
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until the centroids stop moving (or barely move).

Each full iteration can only lower WCSS or leave it unchanged, so the right chart always steps downward and the algorithm is guaranteed to stop. The big circles are centroids.

Part 4: the maths

Distance. Euclidean for points (x1, y1) and (x2, y2): d = √((x2 − x1)² + (y2 − y1)²). Manhattan: d = |x2 − x1| + |y2 − y1|. K-Means uses Euclidean distance.

Update rule. The new centroid of cluster j is the mean of its points: μⱼ = (1 / |Cⱼ|) · Σ x for x in Cⱼ.

Objective. K-Means tries to minimise WCSS = Σⱼ Σ over x in Cⱼ of ‖x − μⱼ‖². Assigning to the nearest centroid minimises it for fixed centroids; moving centroids to the mean minimises it for fixed assignments. Alternating the two can only go down, but it may settle in a local minimum, which is why initialisation matters. Cost per iteration is about n × k × d distance operations, so it scales to big data.

Part 5: the random initialisation trap

If two random starting centroids land in the same blob, the final result can split one real group in two and merge two others. Choose Random in the stepper above and Re-roll to see it happen. K-Means++ picks the first centroid at random, then picks each next one with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centroid already chosen, so the starting centroids end up far apart. Part 6: choosing k

Try k = 1, 2, 3 and so on, and record WCSS for each. WCSS is highest at k = 1 (one centroid, long distances) and keeps falling as k grows, then flattens. The bend is the elbow: pick that k. Always check it against the silhouette score. For a point, a = average distance to its own cluster, b = average distance to the nearest other cluster, and s = (b − a) / max(a, b), between −1 and +1. In the video’s data WCSS falls from about 35,000 at k = 1 and bends at k = 3, where the silhouette is also highest (above 0.8).

What s means for one point

Small a and large b: tight, well separated (near +1). a ≈ b: overlapping (near 0). a larger than b: probably in the wrong cluster (near −1). In scikit-learn, the elbow can be found automatically with KneeLocator (curve convex, direction decreasing).

Part 7: scale your features first

K-Means is all distances, so a feature with big numbers dominates. Salary in rupees (20,000 to 150,000) dwarfs a spending score of 1 to 10. Standardise each feature (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation) before clustering, fitting the scaler on the training data only.

Part 8: where K-Means struggles

  • You must choose k (elbow, silhouette).
  • Round blobs only: it separates clusters with straight lines.
  • Outliers drag centroids, because the mean is sensitive.
  • Local minima: use K-Means++ and several restarts.

For ring-shaped or noisy data, a density method such as DBSCAN does better; that is a different lesson.

Part 9: Python

K-Means from scratch (NumPy)
import numpy as np
def kmeans(X, k, iters=100, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    C = X[rng.choice(len(X), k, replace=False)]              # 1. initialise
    for _ in range(iters):
        d = np.linalg.norm(X[:, None] - C[None], axis=2)      # distance to each centroid
        labels = d.argmin(axis=1)                             # 2. assign
        new = np.array([X[labels == j].mean(axis=0) if (labels == j).any() else C[j]
                        for j in range(k)])                   # 3. update
        if np.allclose(new, C): break
        C = new
    return labels, C, ((X - C[labels]) ** 2).sum()            # WCSS

Part 10: check yourself

Common questions

Is K-Means supervised?

No. There is no target column; labels come from the algorithm itself.

Does it always find the best clustering?

No. It always converges, but possibly to a local minimum. Use K-Means++ and several restarts and keep the lowest WCSS.

What if a cluster ends up empty?

Implementations keep the old centroid, re-seed it at the farthest point, or restart.

Why does WCSS always fall as k grows?

More centroids means every point can sit closer to one. That is why you look for the elbow, not the minimum.

Cheat sheet

  • Initialise (K-Means++), assign to nearest centroid, move centroid to the mean, repeat.
  • Pick k with the elbow of WCSS and confirm with the silhouette score.
  • Scale features first; expect round, similar-sized clusters.
  • fit_predict on training data, predict on new data.
